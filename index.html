Iran Offered to Reopen Hormuz in 7 Days. Trump Said No. Oil Went Up — and So Did Flows.
At the UN General Assembly on Friday 25 September, Tehran proposed reopening the Strait of Hormuz within seven days. In exchange it wanted three things: the US releases frozen funds, lifts sanctions and ends its naval blockade. Iran would then return to nuclear talks. Trump rejected it on Saturday 26 September. US envoy Mike Waltz called it a “pretty cynical attempt.” Since then the signals have diverged:
  • 28 September: Trump confirmed messages are passing through mediators.
  • 29 September: Trump called reports that he had offered sanctions relief “a hoax.” A US official called the talks “constructive” but said nuclear issues are holding up a deal.
  • Iran: FM Araghchi says Iran is ready for a “doomsday war.”
Casualties: the Pentagon's count of US wounded has quietly risen to 861 since 28 February, and at least 19 US service members have died. It is about day 214 of the war.

The part the headlines skip: the strait is carrying more oil, not less. MarineTraffic counted 132 transits in 21–27 September, up from 116 the week before. Kpler puts regional exports at 12.8m b/d, the highest of the war. Official figures disagree badly, though:
  • Energy Secretary Wright: about 13m b/d through the strait.
  • Treasury Secretary Bessent: 15–22m b/d.
  • Kpler: Hormuz crude exports of ~7.4m b/d in September.
That is roughly a 3x spread. The US figures are the ones that make the blockade look like it is working, which is a reason to discount them.

So what: Iran is offering something that is already partly happening — transits are near pre-war weekly levels, against about 130 a day before the war — in exchange for the three things the US values most. That is a weak offer, and Washington was right to read it that way. But turning it down costs oil: Brent rose to $105.28 on Monday, touching $108.83 intraday. Signal to watch: whether the US drops Iran's demand that the June MOU and the Iran–Oman bilateral Hormuz deal serve as the baseline. That is about framing, not substance, and framing concessions are usually how these talks start moving.

Tariffs: Section 122 Expired in July — Correction — and Pharma Tariffs Hit Small Firms Today
Correction to this desk's 8 September edition. We described the 10% Section 122 global tariff as a live “bridge” with a statutory clock still running.
