A 66% Probability of a Fed Hike on 16 September — and Equities Rallied 1%+ Anyway
CME FedWatch put the odds of a 25bp hike at the 15–16 September FOMC at 66% as of 31 August (a separate fed funds futures read gave 60.4%, up from ~56% the prior session). Before Chair Kevin Warsh’s Jackson Hole speech, the odds of no change were near 70% — a complete inversion in a week. Warsh: this summer’s inflation readings “were better than expected” but do not show underlying trends have “meaningfully improved.” The July FOMC held 9–3, with all three dissents favouring a hike. The 10-year eased to 4.76% on 3 September (−3bp; one source prints 4.77%) after touching its highest since 2023. Meanwhile equities ripped: S&P 500 +1.06% to 7,747.71, Nasdaq +1.4% to 26,584.06, Dow +624.16 (+1.18%) to 53,686.11.

So what: A market putting two-in-three odds on a hike does not normally rally 1% across all three indices. One of the two is wrong. The reconciliation most people will reach for is that a hike is now “priced,” but a 66% probability is not a priced event — it is a coin flip with a lean, and the dot plot on 16 September is what actually resolves it. The dissent count is the underrated detail: three governors already voted to hike in July, so Warsh does not need to persuade a hostile committee, he needs two more votes. Treat the equity rally as positioning, not information.

The AI Boom Is Now Visible in the Trade Balance: July Deficit $88.6bn, +24% in One Month, on Record Capital-Goods Imports
BEA/Census reported on 3 September that the July goods-and-services deficit hit $88.6bn, up $17.4bn from $71.2bn in June — the widest since early 2025. Exports $310.7bn (−$6.6bn m/m); imports $399.3bn (+$10.8bn m/m). The goods deficit widened $17.6bn to $119.6bn; the services surplus rose $0.2bn to $31.0bn. The import surge was driven by computers, computer accessories and semiconductors, with capital-goods imports at a record. Year-to-date the deficit is still down $188.4bn, or 29.6%, versus 2025.

So what: The month and the year are telling opposite stories and both are true. Tariffs compressed the 2026 deficit by 29.6% year-to-date; AI capex blew a $17.4bn hole in a single month. That is the central tension in US trade policy right now — the same administration that is tariffing goods to shrink the deficit is presiding over a compute build-out that can only be supplied from abroad. Watch for semiconductors and computing hardware to attract their own Section 232 treatment; the political logic points there, and the drone proclamation shows the template is ready to reuse.

Section 232 Drone Tariffs Took Effect Yesterday — the Thermal-Imaging Carve-In Is the Real Rule
The 13 August proclamation took effect 3 September 2026. Final structure: 25% on drones at or under 25kg takeoff weight without thermal imaging; 100% on anything above 25kg, on any sub-25kg unit carrying thermal imaging, and on docking stations and chargers. Allied caps as previously reported (15% for Japan, Korea, Taiwan, Switzerland, EU; 10% UK). Existing lines unchanged: steel/aluminium/copper 50%, autos 25%.

So what: Correcting yesterday’s framing — the binding line is not weight, it is thermal imaging, which pulls the entire sub-25kg inspection, agriculture and public-safety fleet into the 100% band regardless of mass. That kills the obvious evasion (engineer under 25kg) for exactly the segment where Chinese incumbents are strongest, and it tells you the proclamation was drafted by someone who understood the product. Docking stations and chargers at 100% is the quieter kill shot: it tariffs the fleet-operations layer, where the recurring revenue lives.

💻 Tech & Semiconductors
Confirmed: Nvidia Buys Hugging Face for $12.93bn — the Distribution Layer for Open AI Is Now Owned by a Chip Vendor
Nvidia confirmed on 3 September what had been rumoured since 27 August: it is acquiring Hugging Face for $12.93bn — approximately $11.9bn to investors plus up to $1bn in employee retention. Hugging Face hosts 3m+ models, ~500,000 datasets, 1m+ applications, with 18m+ users and 200,000+ companies. Jensen Huang pledged it will “remain an open platform for the entire AI ecosystem,” with continued support for open-weight models, multi-cloud and multi-accelerator deployment including AMD and Intel. This is Nvidia’s second-largest acquisition ever, after ~$20bn for Groq assets at end-2025.

So what: The open-platform pledge is sincere and largely beside the point. Nvidia is not buying the right to exclude AMD; it is buying the default path — which model card mentions which kernel, which quantisation is one click away, which inference example is the copy-paste. Distribution defaults decide hardware share far more reliably than benchmarks do, and they are almost impossible to litigate. The right question for AMD and Intel is not “will we be delisted” but “who now writes the getting-started guide.” Second-order: at $12.93bn for an unmonetised repository, Nvidia has just set a public price on AI developer distribution, which re-rates every other neutral hub in the stack.

DRC Bundibugyo Outbreak Passes 6,250 Cases and 3,039 Deaths — a 48.6% Case-Fatality Rate
As of 2 September 2026, DRC reported 6,250 confirmed cases of Ebola disease caused by Bundibugyo virus and 3,039 deaths — an increase of 64 cases and 32 deaths in a single day versus the 1 September report. Ituri remains worst-hit with 5,104 cases and 2,324 deaths across 28 of 36 health zones; North Kivu has 888 cases and 601 deaths across 15 of 34 health zones. In Uganda, 20 cases were reported May–June and WHO declared that outbreak over on 25 August after 42 days without a confirmed case. On 1 September WHO issued updated emergency guidance on the potential use of Ervebo®, the only licensed Ebola vaccine, in outbreaks caused by this virus.

So what: Two numbers matter more than the totals. First, the 48.6% CFR — a coin-flip mortality outbreak that has now run past 6,000 cases is an order of magnitude beyond the 2014 West Africa epidemic in lethality per case. Second, the North Kivu ratio: 601 deaths on 888 cases, roughly 68%, versus 46% in Ituri. That gap is almost certainly not viral; it is a proxy for how badly case-finding has collapsed in a conflict zone, meaning North Kivu’s true case count is understated and its true CFR lower. The Ervebo guidance is the operational news — Ervebo is licensed against Zaire ebolavirus, so its use here is off-label and its efficacy against Bundibugyo is not established. WHO issuing guidance anyway is a measure of how few options remain.

💳 Global Payment & Finance Hotspots
Stablecoins Cleared Regulatory Mainstream in Seven Major Economies — They Are Now Payment Instruments, Not Crypto
The US, EU, UK, Singapore, Hong Kong, UAE and Japan now all mandate full reserve backing, licensed issuers and guaranteed redemption rights, treating stablecoins as regulated payment instruments rather than crypto assets. In the US, any stablecoin used for commercial payments must be issued by a licensed, federally supervised entity under reserve, disclosure and AML requirements.

So what: Convergent rules across seven jurisdictions is the precondition for cross-border settlement at scale, and it arrived faster than the industry’s own consensus expected. But read what the convergence actually does: full reserve backing plus federal supervision makes stablecoin issuance a narrow-bank business with narrow-bank economics — the issuer earns the reserve carry and nothing else. That is a rate-sensitive business, not a technology business, and it is precisely the wrong moment to be levered to reserve yield if the Fed is done tightening. The winners will be the distribution layers, not the issuers.

Visa Is Shopping for a Stablecoin Settlement Partner — and Open USD Is the Reason
Visa is publicly seeking a new stablecoin settlement partner with licensing across multiple regions, filling the role previously held by BVNK. So what: Two competing card networks and the largest private payments company co-fronting a single stablecoin is not a crypto story; it is the networks pre-empting disintermediation. If agentic and cross-border commerce migrates to stablecoin settlement, the networks lose interchange — unless they issue the stablecoin. The multi-region licensing requirement is the operative detail: it tells you Visa expects settlement to be regulated locally in each jurisdiction, which favours a small number of licensed intermediaries and makes that licence itself the scarce asset. That is where to look for value, not at the token.

Industry implementation guidance now converges on a consistent timeline for AI fraud and credit deployments in banking: pilot programmes show results in six to twelve months; full deployment takes eighteen to twenty-four months. That has an investment implication: in financial services, the vendor that wins is the one that shortens the validation cycle, not the one with the better model. Explainability Has Become a Documented Compliance Requirement, Not a Technical Preference
Regulatory frameworks now require financial institutions to produce a defensible rationale for AI-driven decisions, elevating explainable AI from a design choice to a documented obligation under model-risk-management standards. This runs alongside the alternative-data credit scoring build-out, where ML models score applications on broader signal sets and automate manual checks that previously took several business days.

So what: This is the constraint that decides which architectures get deployed in regulated lending, and it cuts against the direction the frontier is moving. A large language model cannot currently produce an audit-grade rationale for a credit decline, which means the actual production stack in regulated lending will remain gradient-boosted trees and monotonic constraints with an LLM layered on for narrative generation — not an LLM making the decision. Any vendor pitching end-to-end LLM credit decisioning into a supervised institution is selling something that will not clear model validation. The commercial opportunity is in the documentation and monitoring layer.

JetBrains’ developer research puts Claude Code as the most widely adopted AI coding tool at work, used roughly twice as often as GitHub Copilot. Multi-Agent Is Now a Shipped Pattern, Not a Research Idea: Parallel Agents on Isolated Git Branches
Cursor 3 (April 2026) introduced up to 8 AI agents running in parallel on isolated Git branches, alongside longer-running cloud agents. Across the market, the three dominant approaches have separated by use case: Copilot leads enterprise adoption, Cursor is favoured for agility, and Claude Code is used for the hardest reasoning tasks. A reported acquisition of Cursor by SpaceX remains irreconcilable with contemporaneous reporting and is excluded from this brief pending confirmation.

So what: Isolated Git branches per agent is the important engineering decision and it is worth generalising beyond coding. It solves the coordination problem the cheap way — by removing shared state entirely rather than by trying to make agents negotiate — and it means the merge conflict, not the agent, becomes the integration point. Any multi-agent system that has no equivalent of a branch is going to spend its complexity budget on conflict resolution. For enterprise workflow automation more broadly, the design question to ask a vendor is: what is your branch, and what is your merge?

The Enterprise Reality Check: 80% of Apps Ship With an Agent, 31% of Enterprises Run One in Production
Gartner: 80% of enterprise applications shipped or updated in Q1 2026 embed at least one AI agent, up from 33% in 2024 — but only 31% of enterprises have at least one agent in production, and 89% of agent pilots never reach production. Median time-to-value for those that do is 5.1 months (SDR agents 3.4 months, finance and ops agents 8.9 months). Gartner projects more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by end-2027 from “ungoverned sprawl.” MIT/NANDA attributes ~95% of stalled GenAI pilots to integration failure, not model capability.

So what: The 80%-versus-31% gap is a supply-side push, not a demand-side pull — software vendors are embedding agents because the market expects it, and buyers are not turning them on. That distinction matters for anyone reading “80% adoption” as evidence of anything. The genuinely actionable number is the 3.4 versus 8.9 month split: agents pay back fastest where output is easily measured and errors are cheap (sales development), and slowest where the opposite holds (finance and operations). That is a durable ordering, and it says start where a mistake costs a follow-up email, not a restatement.
