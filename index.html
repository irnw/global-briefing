Iran — Flare-Up Eases: Tehran Privately Calls Ship Attacks a “Mistake” by “Errant Hardliners”; Trump Keeps Truce “Over” but Says US Will Keep Negotiating
The sharpest escalation since June has cooled. After the July 6–7 attacks on three commercial ships in the Strait of Hormuz triggered the most intense US strikes since the mid-June ceasefire, the exchange of fire eased by the evening of Thursday, July 9. Iranian officials privately told Trump advisers the ship attacks were a “mistake” stemming from an “errant” sect of hardliners trying to undermine negotiations, and that Tehran “wants to keep talking.” Trump reiterated the ceasefire is “over” but said the US will continue negotiating under the June 17 Islamabad Memorandum (a 60-day window to finalize terms). Core dispute persists: Iran wants to control Hormuz transit and collect fees; the US rejects it.

So what: De-escalation for now, but a fragile truce with an active war premium still priced into oil. Investment angle: Watch Brent (~$76, up from $70–72 pre-flare) for renewed Hormuz spikes; defense, tankers/shipping-insurance and energy volatility stay favored; reconstruction plays remain deferred.

Trade — Twin Deadlines Loom: Section 122 Global 10% Tariff Expires July 24; USTR Faces July 20 to Finalize Section 301 Replacement (Proposed 12.5% on 46 Countries)
The tariff architecture is being rewritten this month. The 10% Section 122 global tariff expires July 24, and USTR faces a July 20 completion deadline on the Section 301 investigations meant to replace it — with proposed 12.5% duties on 46 countries including China, Vietnam, India, Thailand, Japan and South Korea. The US–EU deal remains live at a 15% all-inclusive ceiling (autos cut from 27.5% to 15%; pharma/semis capped at 15%; steel/aluminum still 50% under Section 232). USMCA stays unrenewed since July 1, entering a decade of annual reviews; Washington meets Mexico bilaterally the week of July 20. Non-EU exporters (notably China) face relative disadvantage and order-diversion risk.

So what: A pivotal 10 days for global tariff structure; walls harden bloc by bloc. Investment angle: Reinforces friend-shoring and pricing-power compounders; import-exposed autos/ag and Asian exporters face fresh uncertainty into the July 20–24 window.

Global Inflation Ticks Up to 4.7% for 2026; Growth Slows as Tariffs and Fragmentation Bite
Global headline inflation is set to rise from 4.1% (2025) to 4.7% (2026) before easing to 3.9% in 2027 (IMF), keeping central banks cautious. Growth is cooling toward ~2.5–2.9% as tariffs, energy shocks and supply-side disruption raise costs (Deloitte/EY/World Bank). Much of the tariff-driven price pass-through still lies ahead — firms have run down pre-tariff inventories — while AI-related investment in North America and Asia provides a partial offset.

So what: A slower, higher-cost, more fragmented expansion with sticky prices. Investment angle: Favor pricing-power compounders, domestic supply chains, real assets and AI capex beneficiaries over rate-sensitive, import-exposed names.
