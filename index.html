Iran Offered to Reopen Hormuz in 7 Days. Trump Said No. Oil Went Up — and So Did Flows.
At the UN General Assembly on Friday 25 September, Tehran proposed reopening the Strait of Hormuz within seven days. In exchange it wanted three things: the US releases frozen funds, lifts sanctions and ends its naval blockade. Iran would then return to nuclear talks. Trump rejected it on Saturday 26 September. US envoy Mike Waltz called it a “pretty cynical attempt.” Since then the signals have diverged:
  • 28 September: Trump confirmed messages are passing through mediators.
  • 29 September: Trump called reports that he had offered sanctions relief “a hoax.” A US official called the talks “constructive” but said nuclear issues are holding up a deal.
  • Iran: FM Araghchi says Iran is ready for a “doomsday war.”
Casualties: the Pentagon's count of US wounded has quietly risen to 861 since 28 February, and at least 19 US service members have died. It is about day 214 of the war.

The part the headlines skip: the strait is carrying more oil, not less. MarineTraffic counted 132 transits in 21–27 September, up from 116 the week before. Kpler puts regional exports at 12.8m b/d, the highest of the war. Official figures disagree badly, though:
  • Energy Secretary Wright: about 13m b/d through the strait.
  • Treasury Secretary Bessent: 15–22m b/d.
  • Kpler: Hormuz crude exports of ~7.4m b/d in September.
That is roughly a 3x spread. The US figures are the ones that make the blockade look like it is working, which is a reason to discount them.

So what: Iran is offering something that is already partly happening — transits are near pre-war weekly levels, against about 130 a day before the war — in exchange for the three things the US values most. That is a weak offer, and Washington was right to read it that way. But turning it down costs oil: Brent rose to $105.28 on Monday, touching $108.83 intraday. Signal to watch: whether the US drops Iran's demand that the June MOU and the Iran–Oman bilateral Hormuz deal serve as the baseline. That is about framing, not substance, and framing concessions are usually how these talks start moving.

Tariffs: Section 122 Expired in July — Correction — and Pharma Tariffs Hit Small Firms Today
Correction to this desk's 8 September edition. We described the 10% Section 122 global tariff as a live “bridge” with a statutory clock still running. In fact it expired on 24 July at its 150-day limit. USTR replaced it the same day with Section 301 “forced-labour” tariffs of 10–12.5% on about 60 economies, which are already being challenged in court (Liberty Justice Center). The core thesis stands: the baseline tariff was re-based under a different statute, not removed. But the fact was wrong by six weeks.

Other tariff news:
  • IEEPA refunds: about $134.7bn of the ~$166bn owed has been accepted for refund, and ~$122bn has gone out. Phase 3 starts 6 October.
  • Pharma: the 100% Section 232 tariff on patented drugs takes effect for smaller manufacturers today, 29 September. It has applied to large ones since 31 July. Firms with an MFN pricing deal and an onshoring plan pay 0% through January 2029.
  • Oracle fiscal Q1: revenue $19.3bn (+30%), remaining performance obligations $664bn, capex $28.5bn, free cash flow −$5.4bn. The stock fell 5.4%.
So what: The pharma tariff is really a pricing policy enforced through tariffs. The 0% exemption for MFN signatories means the tariff's real purpose is to force price deals, and the Washington Post (19 September) found undisclosed terms in those contracts. Mid-size, non-US biotechs without the leverage to negotiate carry the cost. Oracle: $28.5bn of capex in one quarter means annual capex is running above the $70bn guide. Free cash flow is still negative, and the 10-year is now at 5.25%. The question this desk raised — whether AI infrastructure earns its cost of capital — got harder to answer, not easier.

Developer Sentiment: The “46% Distrust” Figure Doing the Rounds Is From 2025
Roundups this month cite 84% AI adoption, 29% trust in AI accuracy, 46% active distrust and 3% “high trust”. These match Stack Overflow's 2025 survey and appear to be recycled as current. The best recent evidence is METR's February 2026 update. METR had to redesign its productivity study because developers now refuse to work without AI, which skews who takes part. Its speed-up estimates have confidence intervals that include zero, meaning inconclusive.

So what: There is still no controlled measurement showing coding agents speed up delivery on real codebases. The only rigorous attempt found that developers won't join the control group — itself a sign of perceived value, but not proof of productivity. Budget decisions should rest on your own before-and-after metrics, not vendor claims or recycled surveys.
